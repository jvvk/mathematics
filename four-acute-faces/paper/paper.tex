\begin{abstract}
Four points chosen independently and uniformly on a sphere span a
tetrahedron. All four of its faces are acute triangles with probability
$1/12$, although each face on its own is acute with probability $1/2$. We
give a short, self-contained proof. Replacing points by their antipodes
produces eight tetrahedra, and an obtuse angle of one of them is a short
vector sum belonging to another. The short sums fill a staircase in a
$4\times4$ grid, and counting what the staircase can look like reduces
the problem to two integrals that Archimedes' hat-box theorem makes
easy. The method first appeared in a publicly shared ChatGPT
conversation. Every step has been checked in Lean.
\end{abstract}

\section{The question}

Choose four points independently and uniformly at random on the unit
sphere. They span a tetrahedron. What is the probability that all four
faces are acute triangles? Here acute refers to the angles of each
triangular face, not to the angles between faces.

A natural first guess goes through one face. Three random points on a
sphere form an acute triangle with probability $1/2$
\cite[Lemma~4]{AEN}. If the four faces behaved independently, the answer
would be $(1/2)^4=1/16$. They do not, since any two faces share an edge,
and the true answer is larger.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm}
Four independent uniform points on the unit sphere in $\R^3$ span a
tetrahedron whose four faces are all acute with probability exactly
$1/12$.
\end{theorem}

The question was asked on MathOverflow and Mathematics Stack
Exchange~\cite{MO,MSE}, where simulations suggested $1/12$. A proof first
appeared in ChatGPT-generated responses in a conversation that Timothy
Chow shared publicly and linked from MathOverflow on 14 August
2026~\cite{ChowChat}. The method below, antipodal flips, the threshold of
Lemma~\ref{lem:threshold} and the count of Section~\ref{sec:count2}, is the
method of that conversation, and no priority is claimed for it. What this
article offers is an exposition: a complete proof that needs only dot
products, a little counting and two one-line integrals.

The plan is short. Section~\ref{sec:angle} turns each angle condition into
a statement about the length of a vector sum. Section~\ref{sec:eight}
flips points to their antipodes and arranges the resulting sums in a grid.
Section~\ref{sec:count} shows that the short sums form a small staircase,
Section~\ref{sec:count2} counts the possible staircases, and
Section~\ref{sec:average} averages.

\section{Obtuse angles are short sums}\label{sec:angle}

Throughout, points of the sphere are unit vectors, and a vector is
\emph{short} if its length is less than $1$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:angle}
For unit vectors $x,y,z$,
\[
 (x-z)\cdot(y-z)=\tfrac12\bigl(\|x+y-z\|^2-1\bigr).
\]
So the angle at $z$ in the triangle $xyz$ is obtuse exactly when $x+y-z$ is
short.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Using $x\cdot x=y\cdot y=z\cdot z=1$, both sides equal
$x\cdot y-x\cdot z-y\cdot z+1$.
\end{proof}

The vector $x+y-z$ is the fourth corner of the parallelogram built on the
two sides of the triangle at $z$. Figure~\ref{fig:angle} shows an example
in a plane through the centre: $x=(1,0,0)$, $y=(0,1,0)$ and
$z=\frac15(3,4,0)$. The angle at $z$ is obtuse, since
$(x-z)\cdot(y-z)=-\frac25$, and accordingly $\|x+y-z\|^2=\frac15$.

The same vector $x+y-z$ is also the sum of the three vertices of the
triangle with vertices $x$, $y$ and $-z$. In the example that triangle is
acute: it contains the centre of its circumscribed circle. So one vector
plays two roles. It tests an angle of one triangle, and it is the vertex
sum of another. The proof keeps track of these two roles.

\section{Eight tetrahedra in a grid}\label{sec:eight}

Let $a,b,c,d$ be the four random points. For every choice of signs
$\sigma=(\sigma_1,\sigma_2,\sigma_3,\sigma_4)\in\{\pm1\}^4$, the points
$\sigma_1a,\sigma_2b,\sigma_3c,\sigma_4d$ span a tetrahedron $T_\sigma$.
Replacing a uniform point by its antipode leaves it uniform, so every
$T_\sigma$ has the same distribution as $T_{(1,1,1,1)}$. The tetrahedra
$T_\sigma$ and $T_{-\sigma}$ are reflections of each other through the
centre and have the same angles, so we count them once. This leaves
eight tetrahedra. Let $N$ be the number of them with four acute faces.
Each has four acute faces with probability $p$, the probability we want, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:EN}
 \E N=8p.
\end{equation}
The eight tetrahedra are far from independent, but~\eqref{eq:EN} needs only
linearity of expectation.

Call a tetrahedron \emph{even} or \emph{odd} according to the parity of the
number of minus signs in $\sigma$. This is unchanged when $\sigma$ is
replaced by $-\sigma$, because there are four signs, so there are four even
and four odd tetrahedra.

A \emph{triangle} here means three of the points $\pm a,\pm b,\pm c,\pm d$
taken from three different pairs, again counted once together with its
reflection. Three of the four pairs can be chosen in four ways, and signs
on the three chosen pairs in four ways up to reflection, so there are
sixteen triangles. Each has a vertex sum, defined up to sign, and so it is
either short or not.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:grid}
Every even tetrahedron shares exactly one face with every odd tetrahedron,
and every triangle arises in this way from exactly one even and one odd
tetrahedron. Two tetrahedra of the same parity share no face.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
A triangle becomes a tetrahedron when a sign is chosen for the point of the
missing pair. The two choices differ in one sign, so one is even and one is
odd. Conversely, sign vectors $\sigma$ of an even and $\tau$ of an odd
tetrahedron differ in one or three places. In the second case replace $\tau$
by $-\tau$, which differs from $\sigma$ in one place. The three places where
they agree give the common face, and there is no other. Two different
tetrahedra of the same parity differ in two places, even after reflection,
and share no face.
\end{proof}

So the sixteen triangles fill a $4\times4$ grid. Put the even tetrahedra
on the rows and the odd ones on the columns, and put in each cell the
common face of its row and its column. A tetrahedron's own four faces are
the four cells of its row, or of its column.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:criterion}
A tetrahedron has four acute faces exactly when every short triangle is
one of its faces, that is, when every short cell lies in its row or column.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Take a face $xyz$ of the tetrahedron. By Lemma~\ref{lem:angle} its angle
at $z$ is obtuse exactly when the triangle $x,y,-z$ is short, and similarly
for the angles at $x$ and $y$. These three triangles and the face $xyz$
are the four triangles on the same three pairs, and the three are not faces
of the tetrahedron. Running over the four faces, the twelve angles of the
tetrahedron correspond one to one to the twelve triangles that are not its
faces. All twelve angles are acute exactly when none of those twelve
triangles is short.
\end{proof}

Thus $N$ counts the rows and columns of the grid that contain every short
cell.

\section{The short cells form a staircase}\label{sec:count}

Which cells are short? Each cell belongs to one row and one column, so it
is natural to look at the two tetrahedra involved. For a tetrahedron $T$
write $S_T$ for the sum of its four signed vertices and $w_T=\|S_T\|^2$.

Take an even tetrahedron $E$ and an odd one $O$. They share a face, with
vertex sum $L$, and their fourth vertices are opposite points $v$ and $-v$.
So, up to sign, $S_E=L+v$ and $S_O=L-v$. The cell's sum $L$ is the midpoint
of the two totals, and the totals differ by $2v$, a vector of length $2$.
The parallelogram law, $\|L+v\|^2+\|L-v\|^2=2\|L\|^2+2\|v\|^2$, turns this
into a statement about lengths.

\begin{lemma}[threshold]\label{lem:threshold}
For an even tetrahedron $E$ and an odd tetrahedron $O$,
$w_E+w_O=2\|L\|^2+2$, where $L$ is the sum of their common face. In
particular their cell is short exactly when $w_E+w_O<4$.
\end{lemma}

So eight numbers, one for each tetrahedron, decide the whole grid.

Order the rows by increasing $w_E$ and the columns by increasing $w_O$.
If a cell is short, then so is every cell above it in its column and every
cell to its left in its row, because moving up or left can only decrease
$w_E+w_O$. The short cells therefore form a staircase in the top left corner
of the grid, a Young diagram. Three more facts fix its size.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:restrict}
\textup{(i)} Among the four triangles on any three pairs, at most one is
short. Hence at most four cells are short.
\textup{(ii)} No tetrahedron has four short faces. Hence no row or column is
entirely short.
\textup{(iii)} At least one cell is short.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
(i) Two of these triangles differ, after reflecting one of them, in the
sign of a single vertex $v$. Their sums are $u+v$ and $u-v$ for some $u$,
and $\|u+v\|^2+\|u-v\|^2=2\|u\|^2+2\ge2$, so they are not both short.

(ii) If a tetrahedron has vertices $x_1,\dots,x_4$ with sum $S$, its faces
have sums $S-x_i$, and
$\sum_i\|S-x_i\|^2=4\|S\|^2-2\|S\|^2+4\ge4$.

(iii) Here, and only here, we use that the space has dimension $3$. Take
a tetrahedron with vertices $x_1,\dots,x_4$ and sum $S$. Its face opposite
$x_i$ has sum $S-x_i$, and
\[
 \|S-x_i\|^2=\|S\|^2-2\,x_i\cdot S+1,
\]
so that face is short when $x_i\cdot S>\frac12\|S\|^2$. Since
$S=\sum_ix_i$, the four dot products $x_i\cdot S$ add up to $\|S\|^2$, so
their average is only $\frac14\|S\|^2$. We need a tetrahedron whose total
can be written with less weight on its vertices. The four points satisfy a
nontrivial linear relation, and this supplies one. Replace by its antipode
each point whose coefficient is negative, and scale so that the largest
coefficient is $1$. After relabelling, the vertices $x_1,\dots,x_4$ of one
of the eight tetrahedra satisfy
\[
 x_1+\lambda_2x_2+\lambda_3x_3+\lambda_4x_4=0,
 \qquad 0\le\lambda_i\le1 .
\]
Taking lengths, $1=\|x_1\|\le\lambda_2+\lambda_3+\lambda_4$. Substituting
for $x_1$, the sum of the vertices is
\[
 S=c_2x_2+c_3x_3+c_4x_4,\qquad c_i=1-\lambda_i\ge0,\qquad
 c_2+c_3+c_4\le2 .
\]
If $S=0$, every face sum $S-x_i$ has length exactly $1$. Otherwise some
$c_i$ is positive, and if every $x_i\cdot S$ were less than
$\frac12\|S\|^2$ we would have
$\|S\|^2=\sum_ic_i\,(x_i\cdot S)<\frac12\|S\|^2\sum_ic_i\le\|S\|^2$, which
is absurd. So some vertex has $x_i\cdot S\ge\frac12\|S\|^2$, and the face
opposite it has $\|S-x_i\|^2\le1$. Lengths exactly equal to $1$
occur with probability zero (Section~\ref{sec:average}), so this face is
short.
\end{proof}

The four orthonormal vectors of $\R^4$ show that (iii) needs the
dimension. Every triangle sum then has squared length $3$, no cell is
short, and all eight tetrahedra are regular.

\section{Counting}\label{sec:count2}

By Lemma~\ref{lem:restrict}(iii) the top left cell is short, so only the
first row and the first column can contain every short cell. The first row
does so exactly when the staircase is a single row, and the first column
exactly when it is a single column. By Lemma~\ref{lem:restrict}(i) and (ii),
the staircase has between one and four cells and no line of four. Up to
interchanging rows and columns, which changes nothing below, there are six
possibilities, listed in Figure~\ref{fig:shapes}.

We cannot average $N$ directly, but we can average anything built from
single cells and pairs of cells in one line. A single cell is short when one
point falls in a cap, and two cells in one row are two faces of one
tetrahedron, sharing two vertices, which will again need only one cap
(Section~\ref{sec:average}). So let $m$ be the number of short cells and
$J$ the number of pairs of short cells in a common row or column, and look
for a formula $N=A+Bm+CJ$. The first three shapes in
Figure~\ref{fig:shapes} give $A+B=2$, $A+2B+C=1$ and $A+3B+3C=1$, so
$A=4$, $B=-2$ and $C=1$. The other three shapes fit the same formula, so
in every case
\begin{equation}\label{eq:count}
 N=4-2m+J .
\end{equation}

\section{Averaging}\label{sec:average}

Each cell is a triangle, and all sixteen triangles have the same
distribution, so each is short with the same probability $\alpha$. A pair of
cells in one row consists of two faces of one tetrahedron, which share two
vertices, like $a+b+c$ and $a+b+d$. All such pairs have the same
distribution, so each is short with the same probability $\gamma$. There
are $8$ lines with $\binom42=6$ pairs each, so taking expectations
in~\eqref{eq:count} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:EN2}
 \E N=4-2\cdot16\alpha+48\gamma .
\end{equation}

Both probabilities come from one fact, Archimedes' hat-box theorem: the
height of a uniform point on the sphere, measured along any fixed axis, is
uniform on $[-1,1]$. Equivalently, a band between two parallel planes at
distance $h$ apart has area $2\pi h$, the same as the matching band on the
circumscribed cylinder. (At height $h$ the circle of latitude has radius
$r=\sqrt{1-h^2}$. A thin band there has circumference $2\pi r$ and sloping
width $dh/r$, so its area is $2\pi\,dh$ whatever the height.)

Fix $a$ and $b$, and let $s=\|a+b\|$ and $u=(a+b)/s$. Then
\[
 \|a+b+c\|^2=s^2+2s\,(c\cdot u)+1,
\]
which is less than $1$ exactly when the height $c\cdot u$ of $c$ along $u$
is below $-s/2$. That is a cap of height $1-s/2$, so
\[
 \Pr\bigl(a+b+c\text{ short}\mid a,b\bigr)=\frac{1-s/2}{2}=\frac{1-t}{2},
 \qquad t=\frac s2 .
\]
Given $a$ and $b$, the points $c$ and $d$ are independent and fall in the
same cap, so both $a+b+c$ and $a+b+d$ are short with probability
$\bigl((1-t)/2\bigr)^2$ (Figure~\ref{fig:cap}). The cap is random, so this
square must be taken before averaging.

It remains to average over $a$ and $b$. Since $s^2=2+2\,a\cdot b$ and
$a\cdot b$ is uniform on $[-1,1]$, again by Archimedes, $t^2=s^2/4$ is uniform
on $[0,1]$. Hence $\E t=\int_0^1\sqrt v\,dv=\frac23$ and $\E t^2=\frac12$, and
\[
 \alpha=\frac{1-\E t}{2}=\frac16,\qquad
 \gamma=\frac{1-2\,\E t+\E t^2}{4}=\frac{1-\frac43+\frac12}{4}=\frac1{24}.
\]
This agrees with the known result for a single face. By
Lemma~\ref{lem:angle}, a given angle of a random triangle on the sphere is
obtuse with probability $\alpha=\frac16$. At most one angle of a triangle
can be obtuse, so the triangle is acute with probability
$1-3\cdot\frac16=\frac12$.

Substituting into~\eqref{eq:EN2},
\[
 \E N=4-\frac{32}{6}+\frac{48}{24}=\frac23,
 \qquad\text{so}\qquad p=\frac{\E N}{8}=\frac1{12}
\]
by~\eqref{eq:EN}. This proves Theorem~\ref{thm}.

Two details were set aside. Given $a$ and $b$ with $s>0$, the squared
length $\|a+b+c\|^2$ is uniform on an interval, so it equals $1$ with
probability zero, and $s=0$ itself has probability zero. The same holds for
every one of the sixteen triangles, so with probability one no vertex sum
has length exactly $1$, no angle is right, and Lemma~\ref{lem:threshold}
has no ties at the threshold. Ties among the $w_T$ do not matter, since any
ordering consistent with the values gives a staircase.

\section{Remarks}

For comparison, Wendel's theorem~\cite{Wendel} gives the probability that
the convex hull of random points contains the centre by the same device of
antipodal flips, but there exactly one flip in each family succeeds. Here
the number of successes $N$ is $0$, $1$ or $2$, which is why a count such
as~\eqref{eq:count} is needed.

A Lean~4 formalisation of Theorem~\ref{thm}, using Mathlib, accompanies the
article as supplementary material. It states the result for the uniform
measure on the sphere and proves it, including Archimedes' theorem and
both integrals, using only Lean's standard axioms. It follows the plan above, although
some steps take different routes: for instance, it proves the count in the
equivalent form $N+C=4$, where $C=2m-J$ is the number of tetrahedra with
one or two short faces, by checking every pattern of short
triangles allowed by restrictions of the kind in Lemma~\ref{lem:restrict}. Sixteen deliberately falsified variants of its
statements fail to compile.
